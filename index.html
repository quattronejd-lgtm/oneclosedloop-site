Chapter 1 · My Crown Fell Out
\ THE COLLISION

Then later ran out.

My crown fell out of my mouth. That's where this story begins.

Not on a scale.
Not in a gym.
Not in front of a mirror under dramatic lighting.

It began with a piece of dental work dropping loose in the middle of an ordinary life that no longer felt ordinary. I'd spent years dancing around the same problem, and I always had a reason to wait. I'll deal with it when business stabilizes. When the kids are older. When there's more money.

There was always a later. Then later ran out.

A hole in my mouth forced me into the dentist's office. The dentist forced me toward the doctor. The doctor forced me toward the truth. And the truth forced me to choose.

Looking back, I don't think the crown falling out was random. I think it was mercy.

You have a later too.

You could name it right now without thinking hard. When the business stabilizes. When the kids are older. When there's more money. Yours has its own wording, but it lives in the same drawer.

Here's what I want you to know: the crisis didn't do the work. It never does. All the crown did was end the waiting. The dentist didn't lose the weight. The doctor didn't change the habits. They just closed the door marked later — and it turns out that was the only door I was hiding behind.

You don't need to fix anything today. I'm not asking for that. I'm asking for something smaller: say the thing out loud. The real thing, the one you've been scheduling for a version of your life that keeps not arriving.

Because one way or another, later ends. You can end it on purpose, gently, this week. Or you can wait for your own crown to fall out.

I'd recommend on purpose. Mercy hurts less when you don't make it chase you.

Sometimes the crisis isn't what breaks you. It's what finally begins you.

\ AWARENESS

The things I told myself.

When the truth finally arrived, I did what people do when it comes before they're ready.

I felt sorry for myself. And then I went looking for somewhere to hide.

I told myself it was hormones.
I told myself I was too busy.
I told myself I had no time to work out.

I ran through every excuse available. Each one felt like a reason. Each one was really just a door I kept closed so I wouldn't have to walk through it.

That's the quiet danger of a good excuse. It sounds like the truth. It even feels like the truth. And it will keep you exactly where you are for as long as you let it.

Now run the same audit on yourself.

Take the sentence you reach for when your thing comes up. You know the one. It arrives fast, sounds reasonable, and ends the conversation.

Here's the test I wish I'd known: a reason points somewhere. An excuse points nowhere. “I can't work out Tuesdays because I have the kids” is a reason, and it comes with a next question — what about Wednesday? “I'm too busy” comes with nothing. It isn't information. It's insulation.

So write your sentence down. Actually write it. An excuse loses most of its power the moment it has to hold still on paper, because you can finally look at it instead of through it.

Then ask it one question: if this sentence disappeared tomorrow, what would I have to do? Whatever you just pictured — that's the door. The sentence was never protecting you from failure. It was protecting you from the doorway.

You don't have to walk through it today. Just stop calling the door a wall.

The story felt like truth. It was just a place to hide.

\ AUDIT

The number I hid from my own wife.

286.4 pounds.

That was the number the scan gave back. Not an attack. Information. But it landed like a verdict, because I had spent years making sure I never had to see it.

I genuinely believed I was at least thirty pounds lighter. That's what avoidance does — it doesn't just hide the number from everyone else. It hides it from you.

I was so ashamed I wouldn't say it out loud. Not to a friend. Not even to my wife. It would take another twenty pounds of progress before I told anyone my real starting number.

That's how private the pain had become. And the strange thing is, the moment the number was finally in the open, it stopped being a judge. It became something I could work with.

You have a number too.

Maybe it's a weight. Maybe it's a bank balance, a blood pressure, a credit score, the real hours on your phone. Whatever it is, you've arranged your life so you never quite have to see it.

I want to be honest about what that costs. Avoidance feels like protection, but the number doesn't pause while you look away. Mine didn't. I lost thirty pounds in my imagination and carried them on my body, and the gap between those two numbers is where the shame lived.

So get your number. This week. Book the scan, open the statement, step on the scale — whatever your version is. Do it on an ordinary morning, without ceremony, and let it be what it is: information.

The number can be bad without you becoming bad. Read that again if you need to. The scale never once judged me. I did the judging, and I did it hardest during the years I refused to look.

You don't have to share it with anyone. I didn't. You just have to know it.

The scale was never the enemy. The not-knowing was.

\ ADJUSTMENT

The easy way out.

I was building a brand around cleaner living. Non-toxic. Intentional. Fewer ingredients.

And I was ready to inject a synthetic drug into my body, because I couldn't see another path.

My wife saw the contradiction before I did.

“So what, you're just going to be on this drug for the rest of your life?”
“Do you really just want to take the easy way out?”
“You've done just about everything under the sun except that.”

Her words stung because some part of me knew they were true. So we made a compromise. I'd try to do it naturally for a week or two first. If I gave it a real effort and failed, I could revisit the drug. At least I would have honestly tried.

I didn't need the shortcut taken off the table forever. I needed one honest week.

Let me be careful here, because this is not a sermon about medication.

Maybe the shortcut is right for you. Some shortcuts are. That call belongs to you — and where it's medical, to you and your doctor. My wife never said never. She asked a sharper question: have you honestly tried?

Notice what that question did. It didn't take the option off the table. It put a small, finite test in front of it. Not forever. Not a new identity. A week or two of real effort, and then decide.

Here's what I want you to know about the honest way: it isn't harder because it's nobler. It's harder because it produces information the shortcut skips. A week of full effort tells you what you're actually capable of, and you can't buy that answer. You can only earn it.

So before your next shortcut, run the compromise. Give the honest way one real week. If you fail with a full effort, take the shortcut with a clear conscience — you'll have honestly tried.

But you might not fail. I didn't.

Sometimes the thing we call the hard way is simply the honest way.

\ THE LOOP

The whole game.

Beneath the shame, something useful was beginning. And it was almost embarrassingly small.

My world narrowed to two goals:

Step on the scale every morning.
Figure out how to make 1,300 calories feel satisfying.

That was the whole game.

Not a grand reinvention.
Not motivational speeches.
Not a new identity overnight.

Just repetition and problem-solving. Look at the truth. Adjust one thing. Do it again tomorrow. I didn't know it yet, but I had just started running the loop that would carry everything after it.

Now pick your two.

Not five. Not a system with colored tabs. Two.

The first is a measurement: one true thing you'll look at every morning. Mine was a scale. Yours might be a bank app, a word count, a minutes-slept number. It has to be a number, and it has to be daily, because daily is what keeps it information instead of drama.

The second is a problem worth solving: how do you make the constraint satisfying? I didn't white-knuckle 1,300 calories. I treated satisfaction as a problem to solve, and the solving is what makes the whole thing survivable. Deprivation you endure. A puzzle you return to.

Notice what's not on the list. Motivation isn't on it. A new identity isn't on it. The game is embarrassingly small on purpose: look at the truth, adjust one thing, do it again tomorrow.

Small is not the compromise. Small is the mechanism. Everything I rebuilt after this started as two goals a discouraged man could actually keep.

Not a bigger life. A smaller one, done honestly.
